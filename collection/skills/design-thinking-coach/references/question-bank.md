# Question Bank

> Load when: starting any design-thinking-coach session. This file is the source of the exact questions, follow-ups, and worked rounds used in every phase.

Questions are grouped by phase and by grade band. Use the band's column, not the student's vocabulary: a quiet grade 9 student may need the grade 5-7 wording, and a confident grade 7 student may earn the grade 8-10 wording. Adjust wording freely; do not adjust the expectation that the student thinks.

---

## How to Ask a Round

- **Number every question.** Students answer "1: ..., 2: ...".
- **One decision per question.** Never stack two questions with "and".
- **Give a starter.** Label it `Starter:` or `One possible answer:`. It exists so nobody freezes, not so the student can agree and move on.
- **Ask for their words.** "Answer in your own words, even roughly." A copied suggestion is not an answer.
- **Offer choices for younger students.** "A, B, or something else?" works better than an open question when a student is stuck.
- **Put the tension in the question for senior students.** Name the trade-off so they must take a side.
- **Wait for the whole round.** Do not chase a single unanswered question before the student has seen the rest.

Round sizes: grades 5-7 use 2-3 questions, grades 8-10 use 3-4, grades 11-12 use 4-5.

## If the Student Has No Idea

Offer three prompts and let them pick one. Do not choose for them and do not invent a problem.

**Grades 5-7**

- What is something at school that wastes time or makes people feel left out?
- What is something at home that adults complain about?
- What is something in your neighbourhood that is broken, missing, or unfair?
- What is a thing your friends always lose, forget, or argue about?

**Grades 8-10**

- What is a job at school that everyone avoids because it is boring or confusing?
- What is a problem you have seen three or more times this year?
- What is something your family pays for that does not work well?
- What is a rule that creates more problems than it solves?
- What is a group in your community that has to work harder than everyone else for the same result?

**Grades 11-12**

- What is a service that works well for one group and badly for another?
- What is a problem you have watched an institution fail to solve?
- What is a cost (time, money, stress, opportunity) that people treat as normal but should not?
- What technology has changed behaviour in a way nobody asked for?
- What is a problem where the people affected are not the people deciding?

## Phase 1: Notice and Define

| Grades 5-7 | Grades 8-10 | Grades 11-12 |
|---|---|---|
| Who has this problem? Picture one real person. | Who exactly has this problem, and how many people around you have it? | Whose problem is this, who carries the cost, and who is currently excluded from existing solutions? |
| When did you last see it happen? What happened? | What do people do today instead, and what does that cost them in time, money, or stress? | What evidence shows the problem is real, frequent, and worth solving? |
| What do they do about it now? | What have you seen, heard, or read that shows this is real? | What would have to be true for this problem to be minor, rare, or already solved? |
| Why do you care about this one? | Is the problem you first described the real problem, or is something underneath it? | Is this a symptom of a different problem? What is the root cause? |
| Finish this sentence: "___ finds it hard to ___ when ___." | Finish: "___ struggles to ___ when ___, because ___." | Write a problem statement naming the user, context, consequence, and affected group. |

**Push further:** "Who else has this problem but is quieter about it?" and "Who does not have this problem at all?" The second question exposes how narrow the user group really is.

---

## Phase 2: Research

| Grades 5-7 | Grades 8-10 | Grades 11-12 |
|---|---|---|
| What do you already know about this? How do you know it? | What do you believe, and what is that belief based on? | What assumptions is the problem statement resting on? |
| What is one thing you need to find out? | What two or three questions do you most need answered? | Which assumptions can be tested with data, and which are value judgements? |
| Who could you ask who knows about this? | Who has already tried to solve this, and what happened? | What existing solutions, pilots, or research address this, and why did they succeed or fail? |
| What did they tell you? What did you see yourself? | What primary source could you gather this week: an interview, a survey, or an observation? | What is the counter-evidence, and who disagrees with the mainstream view? |
| Where could you look it up? Name one place. | What would count as evidence against your idea? | What sample, measure, or comparison would make the evidence credible? |

**Push further:** "What did you expect to find, and did you find it?" and "What changed your mind?" Both turn link-collecting into actual research.

---

## Phase 3: Ideate and Choose

| Grades 5-7 | Grades 8-10 | Grades 11-12 |
|---|---|---|
| What are three different ways to fix it? One must not use an app. | Generate three ideas: one digital, one non-digital, and one "change nothing". | What are the existing alternatives and their failure modes? |
| What would you do with no money and one week? | What does each idea cost in time, money, and skills? | What is the smallest version that still tests the core assumption? |
| What does the person do, step by step? | Which idea can you test within a week? | What are the adoption, maintenance, and cost implications of each? |
| Which idea helps the most people with the least effort? | Who has to say yes for each idea to work? | Which idea creates the least harm and the most fairness? |
| Which one would you actually use? | Which idea fails if the user forgets, has no phone, or cannot read? | Which would you bet on, and what would make you switch? |

**Rule:** do not evaluate the first idea until at least three exist. The no-technology option is mandatory because it is the honest competitor to every app.

---

## Phase 4: Shape the Proposal

Ask one question per proposal section. Do not write the section for them.

| Section | Question |
|---|---|
| One-line pitch | In one sentence, what does this do and who is it for? |
| User | Who is the one person this is for, and what is happening in their day when they need it? |
| Steps | What does the person do first, next, and last? |
| Inputs | What must exist for this to work: people, data, permission, money, devices, a place? |
| Difference | Why is this better than what people do today? What is the evidence? |
| Risks | What could go wrong, technically and socially? |
| Fairness | Who could be harmed, left out, or made to feel watched? |
| Success | What would success look like in four weeks, and how would you measure it? |
| First test | What is the smallest thing you could make or try before building the real thing? |
| Open questions | What do you still not know, and who could answer it? |

**Grades 5-7:** ask for a drawing or a "day in the life" story before asking for a written section. Translate the story into the template afterwards, with the student checking it.

**Grades 11-12:** ask for the assumption behind each claim. A claim without an assumption named is usually a slogan.

---

## Phase 5: Red-Team

| Grades 5-7 | Grades 8-10 | Grades 11-12 |
|---|---|---|
| Pretend you hate this idea. What is the fairest mean thing you could say? | What is the strongest argument against your idea? | Steelman the opposing view. What is the best case against you? |
| What would make you stop using it after one day? | Which assumption, if wrong, breaks the idea? | Which assumption is load-bearing, and what evidence would falsify it? |
| Who might not be allowed to use it, or might not have a phone? | Who is left out by your design? | Who bears the cost, and who captures the benefit? |
| What is one thing you are guessing about? | Why has nobody already done this? | What are the second-order effects and failure modes at scale? |
| What is the smallest thing you could try before making the whole app? | What could go wrong with privacy, safety, or fairness? | What would count as failure in a pilot, and what is the cheapest credible experiment? |
| What would make you give up on this idea? | What would make you abandon it? | Would the world be worse if this succeeded? |

**Do not soften the outcome.** If the idea fails the red-team, say so and help revise it. A proposal that says "test this before building" is a real result.

## Follow-Up Ladder

Use these when an answer is thin, vague, or borrowed. Move down the ladder, not through it.

1. "Say more about that."
2. "Can you give me an example?"
3. "What makes you say that?"
4. "How do you know?"
5. "What are you assuming?"
6. "Who would disagree, and what would they say?"
7. "What would change your mind?"
8. "What is the cheapest way to test that?"
9. "What happens if you are wrong?"
10. "What did you leave out?"

For grades 5-7, stop at 3-6 and use concrete examples. For grades 11-12, questions 7-10 should be routine.

## Worked Rounds

### Grades 5-7, Round 2 (3 questions)

> **1. Who has this problem?**
> Picture one real person. Is it someone at school, someone at home, or someone else?
> *Starter: "A grade 4 student who..."*
>
> **2. When does it happen?**
> Think of the last time you saw it. What was happening just before?
> *Starter: "It happens at lunchtime when..."*
>
> **3. What do they do now?**
> How do they cope with it today, even badly?
> *Starter: "They ask a teacher, or they just..."*

### Grades 8-10, Round 3 (4 questions)

> **1. What do you believe about this problem, and what is that based on?**
> One sentence for the belief, one for where it came from.
> *Starter: "I think most students ..., because I have seen ..."*
>
> **2. Who has already tried to solve it?**
> Name a person, school, app, or organisation. What happened?
> *Starter: "Our school tried ... and it stopped because ..."*
>
> **3. What primary evidence could you gather this week?**
> An interview, a short survey, or ten minutes of observation. Pick one.
> *Starter: "I could ask five people who ..."*
>
> **4. What would count as evidence against your idea?**
> If you found this, you would have to change your mind.
> *Starter: "If most people said they ..., my idea would be weaker."*

### Grades 11-12, Round 4 (5 questions)

> **1. Which assumption is load-bearing?**
> If this one thing is false, the proposal collapses. Name it.
> *Starter: "The proposal assumes that ..."*
>
> **2. What evidence would falsify it?**
> Be specific enough that you would know it when you saw it.
> *Starter: "If fewer than ... of ... , the assumption fails."*
>
> **3. Why has this not been solved already?**
> Market failure, incentive problem, cost, regulation, or nobody cared. Choose and defend.
> *Starter: "The most likely reason is ..., because ..."*
>
> **4. Who bears the cost of your solution, and who captures the benefit?**
> They are often different people.
> *Starter: "The benefit goes to ..., while the cost falls on ..."*
>
> **5. What is the cheapest credible experiment, and what result would make you stop?**
> One week, no code if possible.
> *Starter: "I would ..., and if ..., I would stop."*

## Questions to Avoid

- **"Why?" on its own, repeated.** It reads as an accusation. Use "What made you...?" or "What led to...?".
- **Double-barrelled questions.** "Who is it for and what does it do?" gets one answer, not two. Split them.
- **Leading questions.** "Don't you think this would help?" has a correct answer and teaches nothing.
- **Yes/no questions when you need reasoning.** "Is this a good idea?" produces "yes". Ask "What is the best argument against it?".
- **Abstract questions without an example.** "What is your value proposition?" is not a question a student can answer.
- **Personal or identifying questions.** No age, school, address, contact details, or photos. Grade band only.
- **Questions that do the thinking.** "Would you say the problem is that students forget, or that the school does not remind them?" gives away the analysis. Ask what they noticed instead.
